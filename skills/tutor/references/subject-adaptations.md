# Adapting the Tutoring Flow by Subject

The principles stay the same in every subject: assess first, guide with questions, ask for reasoning, don't give the answer unless asked, review afterward. What changes is what "the answer" and "the work" look like.

## Math
- **Answer:** the final value or proof conclusion.
- **Work:** each step, and *why* each operation was chosen.
- Ask for estimation before calculation ("roughly how big should this be?") to build number sense.
- For proofs, ask "what do you know, and what do you need to show?" before any steps.

## Science (physics, chemistry, biology)
- **Answer:** a value with units, a prediction, or an explanation of a phenomenon.
- **Work:** which principle or law applies, how they set it up, and unit handling.
- Ask for predictions before explanations ("what do you think will happen if…?").
- Probe for common misconceptions (heavier objects fall faster, plants get mass from soil, etc.).
- Many science problems are word problems; use `word-problems.md`.

## History and social studies
- **Answer:** an interpretation, cause, or argument, rather than a single fact.
- **Work:** the evidence and reasoning behind their claim.
- Ask "what evidence supports that?" and "how might someone see it differently?"
- For factual recall, it's fine to let them retrieve the fact; give hints like the time period or related event rather than the fact itself.

## Writing and essays
- **Answer:** the improved thesis, sentence, or paragraph. Don't rewrite it for them.
- **Work:** their reasoning about what makes it stronger.
- Ask questions about their draft: "What's the main point of this paragraph?" "Which sentence does the most work?" "Where might a reader get confused?"
- Point to the location of an issue ("look at the second sentence") rather than fixing it.

## Reading comprehension and literature
- **Answer:** an interpretation or the meaning of a passage.
- **Work:** textual evidence.
- "What in the text makes you think that?" "What does this word choice suggest?"

## Foreign languages
- **Answer:** the correct form, translation, or sentence.
- **Work:** the rule they applied (tense, gender, conjugation pattern).
- When they make an error, ask them to identify the rule: "What tense is needed here, and how is it formed?"
- Give a parallel example with a different verb rather than correcting theirs.

## Coding and computer science
- **Answer:** working code or the bug fix.
- **Work:** their mental model of what the code does.
- "What do you expect this line to do? What does it actually do?"
- Have them trace through the code with a small input.
- Point to the region of the bug, not the fix.

## Economics, finance, statistics
- Treat calculations like math and interpretation like history: ask for the reasoning and what the numbers *mean*.
- "What does this result tell us in plain words?"

## Test and exam prep
- Start with a few diagnostic questions across the topic to find weak spots, then focus there.
- Mix question types and revisit earlier topics later in the session (spacing helps retention).
- End with a short summary of strong areas and areas to keep practicing.
